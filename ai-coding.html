As a developer, I have often been lucky enough to work on greenfield projects.

In these cases, the first step is usually helping the customer or project owner understand what they actually want. They typically have a clear idea of what the program should do, but not the cost or complexity behind each sentence in a specification. AI helps here by suggesting appropriate abstractions that both I, as a developer, and the product owner can understand and reason about. For example I used AI to generate this page, but by the end nearly every line was changed at least once by my hands. It still saved time.

Research and Prototyping:

One of the biggest strengths of AI is how much it improves learning by doing. This year, I migrated the entire user system for a large Norwegian government department from LDAP to Entra ID. I had limited experience with LDAP and had never worked with Entra ID before, yet with AI assistance I was able to prototype solutions in minutes that would normally take days.

I could also query common problems that thousands of users before me had already encountered and get concrete suggestions on how to solve them. Using AI in this way significantly reduced the learning curve—likely by half or more.

Since this experience, my typical process is to start with ChatGPT, then use Grok or Claude to critique and stress test the result. When they stop finding issues, I review the solution myself and add my own critiques. In this way, we move from a basic initial implementation—often full of typical AI mistakes—into something clean and robust.

Many experimental branches are simply deployed, learned from, and then discarded. It is not uncommon to throw away thousands of lines of code after gaining a better understanding of the problem. AI makes it easier to avoid sunk cost fallacy, since the initial investment in a prototype is low. This encourages discarding poor solutions and rebuilding them properly.

Iterative design:

When using AI, it is important to work iteratively. Avoid asking it to solve large, complex problems all at once. Instead, focus on a single concern at a time.

Once a solution is proposed, challenge it. Ask the AI to identify weaknesses, and have other models critique it as well. When only trade-offs remain—and no clear faults—you can be reasonably confident that the concern is well understood and addressed.

It's not always greenfield:

I have also spent considerable time maintaining legacy systems. This is where AI can be especially useful. It can quickly explain what thousands of lines of poorly written code are actually doing, suggest improvements, and often pinpoint the source of bugs.

It can even generate tests for older systems where proper test coverage does not exist. While auto-generated tests are not perfect, they are far better than having no tests at all, and can save both developers and testers a significant amount of time.
