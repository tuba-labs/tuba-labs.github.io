Lessons from an LDAP to Entra ID Migration

As a developer, I have often been fortunate enough to work on greenfield projects. At the end of 2025, I was tasked with migrating the user system for my customer from LDAP to Microsoft Entra ID. The task was scoped to be minimal: keep existing interfaces, keep the existing frontend, and only change reads and writes from LDAP to Entra ID.

At first, it seemed possible. AI could almost perform a one-to-one replacement of the LDAP service with one for Entra ID. However, the structured IDs in LDAP quickly became an issue. The frontend, controllers, and services relied on substring operations on IDs to determine relationships and hierarchy. This approach does not work with Microsoft’s model, where IDs do not encode data.

After spending a week trying to make it work, I decided to start from scratch. I spoke to the product owner and explained why this was a much larger task than initially assumed. With the help of ChatGPT, I was able to clearly communicate the problem. LDAP contains many implicit assumptions in its data model that simply do not map to Entra ID.

In the end, I implemented a snapshot of Entra ID and provided lookup mechanisms to reconstruct inheritance and hierarchy. This allowed most of the frontend to be reused, along with many of the existing services, requiring only minor changes.

I wrote this to remind myself—and to show others—that AI can help with many kinds of problems.

Before Coding:

In cases like this, the first step is often helping the customer or product owner understand what they actually want. They usually have a clear idea of what the system should do, but not the cost or complexity behind each requirement. AI can assist both as an active participant in discussions and by suggesting abstractions that are understandable to both developers and domain experts.

Research and Prototyping:

One of AI’s strengths is how much it improves learning by doing. I had limited experience with LDAP and had never worked with Entra ID before. With AI assistance, I was able to understand its limitations and capabilities, and prototype solutions in minutes that would normally take days.

AI allows you to query common problems that others have already encountered and receive concrete suggestions for solving them. Used this way, it significantly reduces the learning curve.

You can rapidly build and deploy experimental branches, learn what works and what doesn’t, and then discard them. It is not uncommon to throw away thousands of lines of code in a day after gaining a better understanding. AI makes it easier to avoid sunk cost, since the initial investment in a prototype is low. This encourages discarding mediocre solutions and rebuilding properly.

When using AI, it is important to work iteratively. Avoid asking it to solve large, complex problems all at once. Instead, focus on a single concern at a time with as narrow a definition of done as possible.

Once a solution is proposed, challenge it. Ask the AI to identify weaknesses, and have other models critique it as well. When only trade-offs remain—and no clear faults—you can be reasonably confident that the problem is well understood and addressed.

Since this experience, my typical process is to start with ChatGPT, then use Grok or Claude to critique and stress-test the result. When they stop finding issues, I review the solution myself and add my own critiques. In this way, the process evolves from a rough initial implementation—often containing typical AI mistakes—into something more robust.

It's Not Always Greenfield:

I have also spent considerable time maintaining legacy systems. AI is useful here as well. It can quickly explain what thousands of lines of poorly written code are doing, suggest improvements, and often pinpoint the source of bugs—especially when given context about incorrect behaviour.

In one recent project, we had almost no useful logging, making it difficult to understand what was going wrong. A stack trace eventually appeared, pointing to a common "runJob" entry point, but without enough detail to identify which job (among many) caused the issue. I used AI to scan the codebase and add logging and MDC where appropriate, allowing us to trace the problem back to invalid input in an XML definition.

AI can also generate tests for older systems where proper test coverage is lacking. While auto-generated tests are not perfect, they are far better than having none, and they can save both developers and testers significant time.
