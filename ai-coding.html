Before coding:

As a developer, I have often been lucky enough to work on greenfield projects.

In these cases, the first step is usually helping the customer or project owner understand what they actually want. They typically have a clear idea of what the program should do, but not the cost or complexity behind each sentence in a specification. AI can help here both as an active advisor in a meeting and by suggesting appropriate abstractions that both technical people (live devs) and domain people (like product owners) can understand and reason about.

Research and Prototyping:

One of the strengths of AI is how much it improves learning by doing. This year, I migrated the entire user system for a large Norwegian government department from LDAP to Entra ID. I had limited experience with LDAP and had never worked with Entra ID before, yet with AI assistance I was able to understand limitations and capabilities, and prototype solutions in minutes that would normally take days.

AI also let me query common problems that thousands of users before me had already encountered and get concrete suggestions on how to solve them. Using AI in this way significantly reduced the learning curve—likely by half or more.

Many experimental branches are simply deployed, learned from, and then discarded. It is not uncommon to throw away thousands of lines of code after gaining a better understanding of the problem. AI makes it easier to avoid sunk cost, since the initial investment in a prototype is low. This encourages discarding mediocre solutions and rebuilding them properly.

Iterative design:

When using AI, it is important to work iteratively. Avoid asking it to solve large, complex problems all at once. Instead, focus on a single concern at a time with as narrow a definition of done as possible.

Once a solution is proposed, challenge it. Ask the AI to identify weaknesses, and have other models critique it as well. When only trade-offs remain—and no clear faults—you can be reasonably confident that the concern is well understood and addressed.

Since this experience, my typical process is to start with ChatGPT, then use Grok or Claude to critique and stress test the result. When they stop finding issues, I review the solution myself and add my own critiques. In this way, we move from a basic initial implementation—often full of typical AI mistakes—into something clean and robust.

I used AI to generate this page, but by the end nearly every line was changed at least once by my own hands. It still saved time.

It's not always greenfield:

I have also spent considerable time maintaining legacy systems. AI is useful for this too. It can quickly explain what thousands of lines of poorly written spaghetti code are actually doing, suggest improvements, and often pinpoint the source of bugs—especially if you feed it information about the unwanted behaviour.

In one recent project, we had next to no useful logging, making it nearly impossible to understand what was even going wrong. Suddenly a stack trace showed up in an error, which led to a common "runJob" entry but had no information that let us know which job among tens was the cause. I had AI scan the entire codebase and add logging and MDC where appropriate, letting it trace the problem to bad input in an XML definition.

It can even generate tests for older systems where proper test coverage does not exist. While auto-generated tests are not perfect, they are far better than having no tests at all, and can save both developers and testers a significant amount of time.
